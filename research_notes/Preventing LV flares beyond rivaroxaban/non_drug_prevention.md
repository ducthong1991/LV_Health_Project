# Non-drug measures for preventing flares of livedoid vasculopathy (LV)

Research date: 21 November 2026. General information to discuss with a dermatologist, not a treatment recommendation.

Reading notes for the report writer:
- LV is rare and the German S1 guideline itself states that no phase 3 trials are expected. Every non-drug measure below rests on expert opinion, mechanism, or retrospective observation. I found **no controlled study of any non-drug measure with relapse as an endpoint**.
- Verification level is flagged per finding: "(full text read)", "(abstract only)" or "(search-result snippet, not verified in full text)".
- The German S1 guideline was read from the AWMF PDF (German), text-extracted locally. Translations are mine.
- Sources I could not open (HTTP 403 or binary): StatPearls LV chapter, Medicine Today 2014 (Parsi), QxMD abstract of Juan 2006, PubMed page for the Thai study, ClinicalTrials.gov record body, DermNet PRO page.

## 1. Compression therapy in LV without venous reflux

### Takeaway
Guidelines and reviews endorse light compression as an optional supportive measure "if tolerated" (German S1: class I, about 20 mmHg), after arterial disease is excluded, but there are no data showing compression reduces LV relapses, with or without reflux. It is an add-on, not a substitute for antithrombotic treatment.

### Cited Findings
- German S1 guideline (AWMF 013-098, status 01/10/2025, valid to 30/09/2030), section 9.12 "Supportive measures": "Falls durch den Patienten toleriert kann die Durchführung einer Kompressionstherapie der Kompressionsklasse I (KKL I) beziehungsweise mit 20 mmHg erwogen werden." Translation: if tolerated by the patient, compression of class I, or 20 mmHg, can be considered. It is a "can be considered" statement inside a consensus (S1, expert opinion) guideline, with no cited study and no formal numbered recommendation attached (full text read) — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- The same guideline says no phase 3 studies of LV therapies are to be expected, which is why it is consensus-based (full text read) — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- Burg, Mitschang, Goerge, Schneider (Front Med 2022; Münster/Hamburg group who lead the German guideline): "a compression therapy for reduction of edema and stimulation of fibrinolysis seems to be effective after exclusion of an arterial disorder." Framed around co-existing venous insufficiency; no relapse data (full text read via summary) — [Burg et al. 2022, PMC9574051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9574051)
- Seguí and Llamas-Velasco (Front Med 2022): "Compression therapy may also be beneficial, especially in patients with venous insufficiency"; said to reduce oedema and improve ulcer healing. No class, pressure or relapse data given — [Seguí & Llamas-Velasco 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmed.2022.993515)
- Finnish series (Köykkä, Kluger, Isoherranen; J Wound Management 2026;27(2):213-217; 24 patients, Helsinki wound clinic): "Compression therapy is the standard care for every LV patient, if no contraindication." Bandages first, stockings once healed; 11/24 used bandages, 16/24 eventually used stockings. Outcome of compression alone not reported. Note this cohort was older (median 53.5 years) and 58.3% had venous insufficiency, 16.7% peripheral arterial disease, so it does not represent young patients without reflux — [Köykkä et al. 2026](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/jwm/volume-27-number-2/livedoid-vasculopathy-24-finnish-patients-single-center-institution/10.35279/jowm2026.27.02.14)
- French multicentre cohort (Gardette et al., Acta Derm Venereol 2018; 26 patients): authors discuss venous stasis as a contributor and recommend venous compression, especially in summer. Compression use by patients and its effect were not measured — [Gardette et al. 2018](https://www.medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-2965)
- Conde Montero and Vivero (Phlebolymphology 2025, narrative review of atrophie blanche): "Compression therapy must be adjusted to the patient's tolerance, as these lesions are usually very painful." Compression described as a possible adjuvant and preventive measure for ulcerated atrophie blanche with an anti-inflammatory rationale, with the authors noting the lack of research — [Phlebolymphology 2025](https://www.phlebolymphology.org/?p=10662)
- Medicine Today 2014 (Parsi): patients with LV "will benefit from compression stockings"; arterial supply and venous drainage should be assessed before applying compression; "The best grade of compression is class II (20 to 30 mmHg)" (search-result snippet, not verified in full text; page returned 403) — [Medicine Today 2014](https://medicinetoday.com.au/system/files/pdf/medicine_today/article/MT2014-10-054-PARSI.pdf)
- Cutis 2012;90:302-306 (Haunson, Judy, Prall, Miller; case report plus review): "Physical modalities such as leg elevation and compression stockings also are extremely effective." Their 71-year-old patient healed with a silver dressing plus 2-layer knee-high elastic compression wraps. Single case; the "extremely effective" statement cites older reviews, not trials (full text read) — [Cutis 2012 PDF](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/Document/September-2017/090060302.pdf)
- DermNet: compression, leg elevation, bed rest and occlusive dressings "may aid healing" — [DermNet, Livedoid vasculopathy](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/livedoid-vasculopathy)
- Indian series (Gupta et al., IJDVL 2026;92:451-9; 26 patients): leg elevation, tip-toe exercises, consistent compression stockings and avoiding prolonged standing were advised only to the subgroup with chronic venous insufficiency — [Gupta et al. 2026](https://ijdvl.com/treatment-outcomes-in-patients-of-livedoid-vasculopathy-following-antithrombotic-therapy-a-retrospective-observational-study/)
- Mechanism check, "compression stimulates fibrinolysis": the one stocking-specific study surfaced (1989, 17 patients with liposclerosis, 1 and 3 months of stockings) found no significant potentiation of lower-limb fibrinolytic capacity and concluded the benefit of stockings is not via local fibrinolysis (search-result snippet) — [Phlebology 1989 record](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1177/026835558900400305)

### Inferences
- The mmHg figures conflict slightly between sources: German S1 says class I / 20 mmHg; an Australian phlebology article says class II (20-30 mmHg). Compression class definitions differ by country (German KKL I is roughly 18-21 mmHg), so both point to the light end, about 20 mmHg.
- The mechanistic claim that compression "stimulates fibrinolysis" is weaker than reviews imply; the oedema-reduction rationale is better supported, and oedema matters mainly when there is venous disease or visible swelling. In a patient with no reflux and no oedema the expected benefit is unproven.
- Precautions that are consistently stated: exclude arterial disease first (ankle-brachial index is the usual way); titrate to pain tolerance; use bandages rather than stockings over open, painful ulcers; in a hot humid climate tolerance and skin maceration under stockings are practical limits (the climate point is my inference, no LV source addresses it).
- Nothing suggests compression is harmful with rivaroxaban.

### Gaps
- No study, controlled or uncontrolled, reports relapse rates with versus without compression in LV. No study stratifies by presence of venous reflux.
- No LV-specific guidance on how many hours per day, knee-high versus thigh-high, or use during sport.

## 2. Heat, season and climate

### Takeaway
Summer or heat-related worsening is the best-documented trigger in LV: 82-88% of patients with any seasonal pattern in Chinese cohorts, 54% in a French cohort, about 40% of relapses in a Brazilian cohort, but only 1 of 24 in Finland. There is no interventional evidence that cooling, air conditioning or relocation reduces flares; "avoid large temperature swings" is expert opinion only.

### Cited Findings
- Largest cohort (Ban et al., Dermatol Pract Concept 2026;16(3):6930; 358 Chinese patients, Xi'an, 2010-2022): seasonal exacerbation was documented in 183 patients (51.1%); of those, 150 (82.4%) reported summer exacerbation, 21 (11.5%) winter, 2 (1.1%) spring/autumn and 10 (5.5%) no seasonal exacerbation. So summer worsening was recorded in 150/358 = 42% of the whole cohort, and 82% of those with seasonal data (full text read) — [Ban et al. 2026](https://www.dpcj.org/index.php/dpc/article/download/6930/3938/114821)
- Feng, Su, Jin, Shao (Acta Derm Venereol 2014; 24 Chinese patients): "87.5% of the patients (21/24) showed significant summer exacerbation with ulcer formation" (abstract read) — [Feng et al. 2014 abstract](https://medicaljournals.se/acta/content/abstract/10.2340/00015555-1711)
- Gao et al. (Clin Cosmet Investig Dermatol 2025;18:2531-2540; 69 patients, Guangzhou, subtropical): symptom onset occurred in summer in 44.9% (31/69) — [Gao et al. 2025](https://www.dovepress.com/clinical-characteristics-and-disease-course-of-livedoid-vasculopathy-a-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-CCID)
- French cohort (Gardette et al. 2018; 26 patients): warm-weather (summer) flares in 14 patients (54%) — [Gardette et al. 2018](https://www.medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-2965)
- Brazilian cohort (Criado et al., Dermatol Ther 2021; 75 biopsy-confirmed patients, São Paulo, 2005-2019): after treatment, almost 40% of cases relapsed during spring and summer months (abstract, via search result) — [Criado et al. 2021, PubMed 33496999](https://www.pubmed.gov/33496999/)
- Finnish cohort (Köykkä et al. 2026; 24 patients): only 1/24 had a seasonal (summer) exacerbation; the authors attribute this to Finland's mild summers and stable indoor temperatures — [Köykkä et al. 2026](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/jwm/volume-27-number-2/livedoid-vasculopathy-24-finnish-patients-single-center-institution/10.35279/jowm2026.27.02.14)
- Rivaroxaban long-term study (Deng, Liu, Huang, Shi; JAAD Int 2023;14:36-38; 34 patients, Changsha): 4 patients had mild-to-moderate relapses triggered by heat exposure while on treatment; 3 patients had mild summer relapses off treatment, declined retreatment, and improved as temperatures fell: "mild relapses after discontinuation of the medication appear to resolve spontaneously as the temperature drops." Authors conclude "the risk of LV relapse is heightened during the summer" — [Deng et al. 2023, PMC10897505](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10897505/)
- German S1 guideline 2025: symptoms "can fluctuate seasonally, in some patients there is worsening in the summer months", but it stresses LV is a year-round disease; clusters in spring/summer, in winter, and year-round manifestation are all described; summer occurrence is not required for diagnosis. Its summary adds that classic summer ulceration represents only part of LV patients and the disease, "perhaps in the context of climate change", is to be treated as occurring all year — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- German S1 guideline, supportive measures: "Zudem kann versucht werden auf große Temperaturschwankungen zu verzichten" (one can also try to avoid large temperature fluctuations), linked in the text to stasis. No cited evidence — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- Seguí and Llamas-Velasco 2022: increased ambient temperature is cited as a trigger; "avoidance of massive temperature changes" is recommended; clinicians should suspect LV in "recurrent small painful ulcerations mainly around the ankles when the temperature rises" — [Seguí & Llamas-Velasco 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmed.2022.993515)
- Burg et al. 2022: "LV occurs perennial, not seasonal", while noting some authors "describe exacerbation during warm weather seasons" — [Burg et al. 2022, PMC9574051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9574051)
- DermNet: the condition often becomes more frequent during the summer months and during pregnancy — [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/livedoid-vasculopathy)
- Tropical data: Thai cohort (Rujitharanawong et al., Dermatol Ther 2021;34(1):e14569; 74 patients, Siriraj Hospital, Bangkok) and Indian cohort (Gupta et al. 2026; 26 patients, Bhubaneswar) exist, but neither abstract/summary reports seasonal variation or heat as a trigger — [Rujitharanawong et al. 2021](https://murex.mahidol.ac.th/en/publications/livedoid-vasculopathy-clinical-course-and-long-term-outcome-in-as/); [Gupta et al. 2026](https://ijdvl.com/treatment-outcomes-in-patients-of-livedoid-vasculopathy-following-antithrombotic-therapy-a-retrospective-observational-study/)
- Pathogenesis note in the German guideline that runs the other way: the lower leg is said to be predisposed because of higher perfusion pressure and lower temperature there, giving lower concentrations of thrombolytic factors. Ban et al. also note that the livedo discolouration intensifies with cold exposure or prolonged standing — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf); [Ban et al. 2026](https://www.dpcj.org/index.php/dpc/article/download/6930/3938/114821)

### Inferences
- The gradient across cohorts (Finland about 4%, France 54%, China 82-88% of those with a pattern) plus the Deng observation that relapses settled as temperatures fell is consistent with ambient heat being a real, dose-dependent trigger. It is all retrospective and self-reported, and studies used different denominators, so the percentages are not directly comparable.
- For a person in an equatorial climate there is no cool season, so the "summer flare" literature implies permanently elevated baseline risk rather than a predictable off-season. This is an inference; no study examines year-round tropical exposure or compares the same patients in different climates.
- The Finnish authors' remark about stable indoor temperatures is the only hint that a controlled indoor climate matters, and it is speculation by the authors, not a tested intervention.
- Because both heat (vasodilation, venous pooling, stasis) and cold (lower fibrinolytic activity, livedo intensification) are implicated by different sources, the guideline wording "avoid large temperature swings" is more defensible than "keep the legs as cold as possible". Aggressive local cooling (ice) has no supporting data and a plausible downside.

### Gaps
- No study of cooling strategies, air conditioning, cold-water immersion, or relocation as an intervention for LV. None found for Singapore specifically.
- No study relates flares to measured temperature or humidity, and none separates heat itself from correlated summer behaviours (more standing or walking, open footwear and minor trauma, dehydration, insect bites).
- Mechanism of summer worsening is not established in any source read.

## 3. Standing, sitting, exercise and the calf muscle pump

### Takeaway
Prolonged standing is reported as an aggravating factor in one small series (12 of 24 patients) and stasis is accepted as a pathogenic co-factor, but there is no LV-specific evidence that exercise is protective or that any exercise type is harmful. Advice to avoid long standing, elevate the legs and stay active is borrowed from venous disease.

### Cited Findings
- Feng et al. 2014 (24 Chinese patients): in 12 patients the disease was exacerbated after a long period of standing (search-result summary of the full text; not in the abstract and not verified by me in full text) — [Feng et al. 2014](https://medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-1711)
- Ban et al. 2026: livedo discolouration "intensifies with cold exposure or prolonged standing" (statement about the livedo pattern, not about ulcer flares) — [Ban et al. 2026](https://www.dpcj.org/index.php/dpc/article/download/6930/3938/114821)
- Seguí and Llamas-Velasco 2022: LV may be associated with "any conditions related to stasis"; local stasis is considered important in pathogenesis — [Seguí & Llamas-Velasco 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmed.2022.993515)
- German S1 guideline: higher perfusion pressure in the lower limb is named as a reason LV localises there. The supportive-measures section contains no recommendation on standing, sitting, exercise or leg elevation — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- French cohort (Gardette et al. 2018): physical exertion triggered flares in 1 of 26 patients. This is the only report found of exercise as a trigger — [Gardette et al. 2018](https://www.medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-2965)
- Conde Montero and Vivero 2025 (atrophie blanche review, mainly venous context): "leg elevation, regular physical activity, and weight management, are also recommended to reduce venous hyperpressure" — [Phlebolymphology 2025](https://www.phlebolymphology.org/?p=10662)
- Gupta et al. 2026: tip-toe exercises, leg elevation and avoidance of prolonged standing advised to LV patients with chronic venous insufficiency; effect not analysed — [Gupta et al. 2026](https://ijdvl.com/treatment-outcomes-in-patients-of-livedoid-vasculopathy-following-antithrombotic-therapy-a-retrospective-observational-study/)
- Cutis 2012 and DermNet list leg elevation (and bed rest during active ulceration) among helpful physical measures, without data — [Cutis 2012 PDF](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/Document/September-2017/090060302.pdf); [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/livedoid-vasculopathy)
- Sport on an anticoagulant: traditional guidance advises athletes on long-term anticoagulation against contact and collision sports; non-contact sport is considered safe. More recent expert opinion favours individualised decisions, including timing DOAC doses so that drug level is low during play; all of this is expert opinion in athletes anticoagulated for venous thromboembolism, not LV — [ACC 2023, Antithrombotic Therapy in Athletes](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2023/08/31/11/58/antithrombotic-therapy-in-athletes); [Review, PMC6246004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6246004)

### Inferences
- The calf-pump rationale (walking lowers ambulatory venous pressure and stasis) is physiologically sound, but in LV it is extrapolation. No source separates "movement" from "time upright in the heat".
- Football combines three things the LV literature flags separately: heat, repeated minor trauma to ankles and feet, and (on rivaroxaban) bruising and bleeding risk. No LV paper addresses football or contact sport; the concern is inferred. Gym work and non-contact aerobic exercise have no reported downside in LV apart from the single exertion-triggered patient in the French series.
- The "move every 30 minutes" rule has no LV-specific evidence base at all; it is reasonable general anti-stasis advice.

### Gaps
- No study of exercise programmes, step counts, sedentary time or occupation (standing versus desk work) in relation to LV flares.
- No data on specific exercise types (running, resistance training, swimming, cycling) in LV.

## 4. Trauma and footwear

### Takeaway
Protection from knocks and ill-fitting shoes is standard expert advice (DermNet), but the evidence that minor injury triggers LV lesions is limited to anecdote: 2 of 24 patients in one series. I found no description of true Koebner or pathergy behaviour in LV in the sources read.

### Cited Findings
- DermNet general measures: protect the legs and feet from knocks, abrasions, known allergens or irritants, and ill-fitting shoes — [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/livedoid-vasculopathy)
- Feng et al. 2014: 2 of 24 patients had a history of trauma to the legs and feet before development of LV in those regions (search-result summary of the full text, not verified in full text) — [Feng et al. 2014](https://medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-1711)
- Burg et al. 2022 do not identify trauma as an LV trigger; trauma appears only as a general cause of hypercoagulability alongside surgery and hospitalisation — [Burg et al. 2022, PMC9574051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9574051)
- German S1 guideline: no statement on trauma or footwear. It notes that the disease favours the malleolar region and that reduced perfusion impairs wound healing and favours wound infection — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- Conde Montero and Vivero 2025: no trauma-avoidance recommendation for LV; minor trauma is mentioned as a trigger only for hydroxyurea-related ulcers — [Phlebolymphology 2025](https://www.phlebolymphology.org/?p=10662)

### Inferences
- The rationale for protection is mainly that atrophie blanche skin is thin, poorly perfused and heals slowly, so any break in the skin over the ankle can become a chronic wound, rather than that trauma switches on the thrombotic process. That distinction is my reading; no source tests it.
- Practical corollaries (well-fitting closed shoes, padding or shin/ankle guards for sport, avoiding friction over the malleoli, care with nail and callus work) are sensible extensions of the DermNet advice but have no LV data.
- On an anticoagulant, a knock to scarred ankle skin is more likely to bruise or bleed into the tissue, which is a further reason for protection.

### Gaps
- No systematic data on trauma as a trigger, and no report found that documents new LV lesions arising in fresh surgical scars, biopsy sites or tattoos.
- No footwear or orthotic study in LV.

## 5. Smoking, nicotine, vaping, alcohol and caffeine

### Takeaway
Smoking cessation is the non-drug measure with the most suggestive LV-specific signal: 42% of a French cohort smoked and two patients in that cohort reached long-term remission after stopping smoking with no maintenance drug. It is still uncontrolled, very small-number evidence. Nothing was found on vaping, nicotine replacement, alcohol or caffeine in LV.

### Cited Findings
- French cohort (Gardette et al. 2018; 26 patients): 42% were smokers, versus 32-35% in the French general population (no formal control group). Of 9 patients in long-term remission (no flare for at least 4 years), 5 had no maintenance treatment, and two of these remitted after smoking cessation alone (full text read via summary) — [Gardette et al. 2018](https://www.medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-2965)
- University of Zurich observational study registered as "Livedoid Vasculopathy: Strong Association With Smoking, Weak Association With Thrombophilia" (NCT05878327): LV patients from Zurich, Basel and Bern, smoking history and thrombophilia compared with the Swiss general population; 15 patients; status completed. No published results were found, so the "strong association" is the registrants' title, not verified data (search-result snippet; registry page body could not be read) — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05878327](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05878327)
- Seguí and Llamas-Velasco 2022: "Cessation of smoking is a crucial preventive measure as vasoconstriction and hypoxia may increase tissue damage"; the review states smokers were more common among LV patients than controls and that patients "should be advised to enter a smoking cessation program". I could not locate the primary controlled study behind the comparison — [Seguí & Llamas-Velasco 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmed.2022.993515)
- Burg et al. 2022: it is "presumed, that smoking cessation could have a positive effect for the disease" — [Burg et al. 2022, PMC9574051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9574051)
- Cutis 2012: "Smoking cessation also should be encouraged"; DermNet: stop smoking because it reduces peripheral blood flow; Conde Montero 2025: "Cessation of smoking may also be beneficial" — [Cutis 2012 PDF](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/Document/September-2017/090060302.pdf); [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/livedoid-vasculopathy); [Phlebolymphology 2025](https://www.phlebolymphology.org/?p=10662)
- German S1 guideline 2025: the supportive-measures section does not mention smoking or nicotine at all (text searched for Rauch/Nikotin/Tabak) — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- Finnish cohort: smoking status was so poorly documented that prevalence could not be reported; authors call for structured recording of smoking — [Köykkä et al. 2026](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/jwm/volume-27-number-2/livedoid-vasculopathy-24-finnish-patients-single-center-institution/10.35279/jowm2026.27.02.14)

### Inferences
- For a non-smoker, this section offers no additional lever; the relevant message is to stay away from all nicotine sources. Because the proposed mechanism is nicotine-mediated vasoconstriction and hypoxia, it is reasonable (but unproven) to extend the caution to vaping, nicotine pouches and passive smoke.
- Alcohol: no LV data. The practical issue is interaction with anticoagulation and injury risk rather than LV activity (inference, no source).
- Caffeine: no LV data and no source suggests it matters.

### Gaps
- No published case-control or cohort analysis quantifying smoking as a risk factor for LV onset or relapse (the Zurich study appears unpublished as of this search).
- No data on vaping, nicotine replacement, cannabis, alcohol or caffeine in LV.

## 6. Body weight, blood pressure, glucose, lipids and lipoprotein(a)

### Takeaway
Raised BMI and hypertension are described as co-factors and elevated lipoprotein(a) is found in roughly 40% of tested patients, but no study shows that LV activity tracks these factors or that correcting them reduces flares. Lp(a) is genetically determined and not meaningfully changed by lifestyle.

### Cited Findings
- German S1 guideline 2025: "Als Kofaktoren sind ein erhöhter Body-Mass-Index sowie arterielle Hypertonie beschrieben" (raised BMI and arterial hypertension are described as co-factors), followed by the statement that no specific comorbidities have been identified that are associated with more frequent LV compared with the general population — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- Weishaupt et al. (J Eur Acad Dermatol Venereol 2019;33(9):1784-1791; 27 patients from the German rivaroxaban phase IIa trial population): increased BMI in 11/27, hypertension in 19/27, raised lipoprotein(a) in 5/12 tested, raised homocysteine in 10/12 tested; median age 53 (abstract only; no control group described) — [Weishaupt et al. 2019](https://read.qxmd.com/read/31009111/characteristics-risk-factors-and-treatment-reality-in-livedoid-vasculopathy-a-multi-centre-analysis)
- Criado et al. 2021 (75 Brazilian patients): about 66% had associated thrombophilia factors; lipoprotein(a) was the most common, raised in 30 of 72 tested (41.66%) (abstract and secondary citation) — [Criado et al. 2021, PubMed 33496999](https://www.pubmed.gov/33496999/); [Seguí & Llamas-Velasco 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmed.2022.993515)
- German S1 guideline: lipoprotein(a) resembles plasminogen structurally and so has anti-fibrinolytic properties, raising thrombotic tendency; it is included in the recommended laboratory work-up. Fewer than half of patients have any identifiable procoagulant abnormality — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- Burg et al. 2022: Lp(a) is "an independent, genetically determined and not life-style-driven risk factor for cardiovascular diseases"; obesity is among the most reported comorbidities in LV and a general cause of hypercoagulability — [Burg et al. 2022, PMC9574051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9574051)
- Gao et al. 2025 (69 Chinese patients, median age 31): hypertension 13.0%, diabetes 5.8%, hyperlipidaemia 23.2%, hepatic steatosis 20.3%, lower-limb atherosclerosis 20.3%; elevated LDL in 48.3% of those tested; 21.7% received lipid-lowering therapy. No analysis linking these to disease activity or relapse — [Gao et al. 2025](https://www.dovepress.com/clinical-characteristics-and-disease-course-of-livedoid-vasculopathy-a-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-CCID)
- French cohort: BMI was collected but no BMI or obesity result is reported; one patient with familial hypertriglyceridaemia — [Gardette et al. 2018](https://www.medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-2965)
- Seguí and Llamas-Velasco 2022 list "control of cardiovascular risk factors" among the main management strategies alongside pain management, wound care and anticoagulants — [Seguí & Llamas-Velasco 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmed.2022.993515)
- German S1 guideline, Recommendation 11: with hyperhomocysteinaemia, vitamin B6, B12 and folic acid should be given in addition (off label). Listed here because it is a nutrient rather than a drug, but it is an add-on to anticoagulation and applies only when homocysteine is raised — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- Diet: a single BMJ Case Reports paper (2021) describes a 63-year-old woman in New Zealand whose long-standing LV remitted on a whole-foods plant-based diet and recurred several times with poor dietary adherence. The authors themselves stress it is one case and that LV can clear spontaneously; two authors declared an interest in plant-based diet resources — [BMJ Case Reports 2021;14:e237895](https://casereports.bmj.com/content/14/2/e237895)

### Inferences
- The metabolic associations come from uncontrolled descriptive series, several in older European patients (median age 53) in whom hypertension and raised BMI are common anyway. The guideline's own second sentence effectively says the association is not established.
- Keeping weight, blood pressure, glucose and LDL in range is justified on general cardiovascular grounds and because obesity raises venous pressure and thrombotic tendency, but there is no evidence it changes LV flare frequency.
- If Lp(a) is high, lifestyle will not lower it. It is worth knowing as a marker (it is in the guideline work-up), and the only LV-specific responses reported are drugs (danazol case reports), which are outside the scope of these notes.
- Checking homocysteine is the one laboratory finding with a cheap non-prescription response (B vitamins and folate), but only as an add-on and with low-quality evidence.

### Gaps
- No study correlating BMI, blood pressure, HbA1c, LDL or Lp(a) with LV flare frequency, and no intervention study (weight loss, statin, antihypertensive) with LV outcomes.
- No data on hydration status or dehydration as a trigger, despite its plausibility in hot climates.
- Diet evidence is a single case report.

## 7. Hyperbaric oxygen, intermittent pneumatic compression and other devices

### Takeaway
Hyperbaric oxygen (HBO) can heal active LV ulcers and relieve pain quickly in small uncontrolled series, but relapses after stopping are frequent (about six months later in the main series), so it is a rescue treatment and not a prevention strategy. No LV data exist for intermittent pneumatic compression. PUVA has only case-level support.

### Cited Findings
- German S1 guideline 2025, section 9.11: only individual case reports support HBO (off label). Regimen in those reports: 5 sessions per week of 90 minutes over 2-5 weeks; pain reduction after 1 week, healing of active lesions after 3-4 weeks; "after the end of HBO therapy relapse occurred in some patients after about 6 months". Summary: time- and cost-intensive, fast pain relief, but treatment success "in part only of short duration" — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- Juan et al. (Br J Dermatol 2006;154:251-255; Taiwan): 12 patients with active LV, HBO five times a week; 8 completed treatment; six patients suffered relapses of ulceration and responded to additional HBO. Authors called HBO relatively safe, fast and effective (abstract via search result; full text not read) — [Juan et al. 2006 record](https://pure.lib.cgu.edu.tw/en/publications/livedoid-vasculopathy-long-term-follow-up-results-following-hyper/)
- A 2015 single case report (Indian Journal of Dermatology) suggested HBO might give longer recurrence-free periods as well as healing; one patient only (search-result snippet) — [Indian J Dermatol 2015 record](https://doaj.org/article/d32806692eb041d69ceffd0b5131d884)
- A 2023 case report describes refractory LV with an MTHFR polymorphism treated successfully with HBO (search-result snippet) — [PMC10608380](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10608380)
- Gao et al. 2025: HBO was used in 7/69 patients (10.1%) as part of multimodal inpatient treatment; its separate effect is not reported — [Gao et al. 2025](https://www.dovepress.com/clinical-characteristics-and-disease-course-of-livedoid-vasculopathy-a-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-CCID)
- Seguí and Llamas-Velasco 2022: HBO increases tissue oxygenation and "promotes fibrinolysis and angiogenesis"; reserved for refractory cases because of cost and compliance — [Seguí & Llamas-Velasco 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmed.2022.993515)
- PUVA: the German guideline cites single case reports of success with systemic 8-MOP PUVA (off label), with an immunological mechanism proposed. In the Thai cohort, add-on anticoagulant or PUVA improved disease in most patients so treated (abstract only) — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf); [Rujitharanawong et al. 2021](https://murex.mahidol.ac.th/en/publications/livedoid-vasculopathy-clinical-course-and-long-term-outcome-in-as/)
- Intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC): no LV study found. In non-LV populations the evidence that IPC enhances fibrinolysis is mixed: a 1997 Annals of Surgery study attributed increased fibrinolytic activity to reduced PAI-1 and increased tPA activity, while later studies in healthy volunteers and surgical patients found no significant change (search-result snippets) — [Annals of Surgery 1997, PMC1191029](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1191029)
- The German guideline's therapy overview table also lists leeches ("Blutegel") among other reported options, without any supporting text in the body of the guideline — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)

### Inferences
- HBO has no role in keeping a healed patient in remission: the only follow-up data show relapse in the majority after a course, and maintenance HBO has not been studied. Cost and time (10-25 sessions of 90 minutes) make it unrealistic as prophylaxis.
- IPC is biologically interesting in LV (possible fibrinolytic effect plus anti-stasis) but is entirely untested; it would be painful over active ulcers.
- Nothing suggests HBO or PUVA is unsafe with rivaroxaban; PUVA carries its own long-term skin cancer risk and is a drug-plus-light treatment rather than a pure non-drug measure.

### Gaps
- No controlled trial of HBO in LV; no data on maintenance or seasonal "pre-emptive" HBO courses.
- No LV data on IPC, neuromuscular electrical stimulation of the calf, low-level laser/photobiomodulation or infrared therapy were found in this search.

## 8. Skin care and wound care at atrophie blanche sites

### Takeaway
Wound care advice in LV is generic chronic-wound practice (gentle cleansing, moist or occlusive dressings, treat infection, minimal dressing changes because of pain), and no skin-care practice has been shown to reduce recurrence at old scar sites. The most LV-specific and potentially useful non-drug practice is early recognition of the pain prodrome ("angina cutis") with a pain diary, which is a trigger to escalate drug treatment rather than a replacement for it.

### Cited Findings
- German S1 guideline: new ulcers are typically preceded by localised burning pain ("angina cutis"); "between angina cutis (prodromal phase) and the appearance of necrosis there are usually 1-3 days. In this window tissue necrosis can often still be prevented by rapid initiation of therapy." Recommendation 6 (expert consensus score +1.9, near the top of the guideline's agreement scale): because worsening is usually preceded by increasing pain, a dose increase of anticoagulation should be considered as soon as pain clearly increases, and a pain diary is helpful for monitoring — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- German S1 guideline, section 9.13: wound therapy should follow the German S3 wound guideline (DGfW 2023); pain therapy follows the WHO ladder; antibiotics only in the rare case of bacterial superinfection. No LV-specific dressing or emollient advice — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- DermNet: remove slough and dead tissue from ulcers; treat infection (cellulitis) with antibiotics; occlusive wound dressings with elevation, compression and bed rest may aid healing; protect from irritants and allergens. After remission, atrophie blanche patches become less defined over time — [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/livedoid-vasculopathy)
- Conde Montero and Vivero 2025: "minimize dressing changes as much as possible and perform only minimal wound cleansing" because lesions are very painful; ulcerated atrophie blanche is treated as a hard-to-heal wound; punch grafting with compression may speed healing and reduce pain, and in the authors' own observation perilesional atrophie blanche reversed after punch grafting (authors' experience, not a study) — [Phlebolymphology 2025](https://www.phlebolymphology.org/?p=10662)
- Cutis 2012: basic wound care with zinc oxide, glycerin or gelatin bandages (Unna-boot type) should be employed; "Proper wound care in patients with livedoid vasculopathy is essential" — [Cutis 2012 PDF](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/Document/September-2017/090060302.pdf)
- Seguí and Llamas-Velasco 2022 mention topical "perfusion-promoting formulations" as a preventive measure, without specifying products or citing outcome data — [Seguí & Llamas-Velasco 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmed.2022.993515)
- Burg et al. 2022: pain relief is "often achieved by usage of anticoagulants as well as an appropriate wound care" — [Burg et al. 2022, PMC9574051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9574051)
- German S1 guideline: non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs have no causal benefit in LV but "can be considered" for supportive pain relief — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)

### Inferences
- A pain diary plus a pre-agreed plan with the dermatologist for what to do at the first burning pain (the guideline suggests dose escalation) is the closest thing to an evidence-informed self-management tool. It supports intermittent or stepped drug use; it does not remove the need for a drug.
- Emollients, sun protection of scars, avoiding adhesive tapes and harsh antiseptics on atrophie blanche skin, and treating tinea pedis or eczema promptly so the skin barrier at the ankle stays intact are reasonable general skin-care practices, but none was found in an LV source and none has recurrence data.
- Caution with an anticoagulant: sharp debridement and punch grafting carry bleeding risk on rivaroxaban and should be planned by the treating team. Regular NSAID use for pain adds to bleeding risk with rivaroxaban (general pharmacology, not from an LV source), so the guideline's "NSAIDs can be considered" needs to be read in that light.

### Gaps
- No trial or series of any topical or skin-care regimen with recurrence at atrophie blanche sites as an outcome.
- Wound-society guidance specific to atrophie blanche or LV was not found beyond the German S3 general wound guideline that the S1 defers to.

## 9. Stress, sleep, infections and vaccinations as triggers

### Takeaway
Infection has weak support as a trigger (3 of 24 patients in one series reported worsening after upper respiratory infection, plus COVID-19 case reports). COVID-19 vaccination has isolated case reports only. Pregnancy is named by DermNet. No evidence was found for psychological stress or sleep.

### Cited Findings
- Feng et al. 2014: in 3 of 24 patients symptoms were worse after upper respiratory tract infection (search-result summary of the full text, not verified in full text) — [Feng et al. 2014](https://medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-1711)
- Burg et al. 2022: "an exacerbation and a relapse of LV were reported after an infection with COVID-19"; the authors state that the specific trigger tipping the haemostatic balance in LV is unknown — [Burg et al. 2022, PMC9574051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9574051)
- A 2022 JAAD Case Reports paper describes LV due to secondary cryofibrinogenaemia associated with COVID-19 that recrudesced during a later non-SARS-CoV-2 infection despite anticoagulation (search-result snippet; exact URL among the results not confirmed, likely one of the PMC records returned) — [Search result record, PMC9445267](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9445267)
- Vaccination: a 2026 case report describes a 65-year-old man diagnosed with livedoid vasculitis 5 days after a third COVID-19 vaccine dose; causation is the authors' hypothesis (search-result snippet) — [Drug Healthc Patient Saf 2026 case report](https://www.dovepress.com/exosome-therapy-for-livedoid-vasculitis-following-covid-19-vaccination-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-DHPS)
- Feng et al. 2014 also reported that most antiphospholipid-positive patients tested (10/14, 71.4%) were hypersensitive to insect antigens, and proposed non-procoagulant factors in pathogenesis; this is a single-centre observation that has not been replicated in the sources read — [Feng et al. 2014 abstract](https://medicaljournals.se/acta/content/abstract/10.2340/00015555-1711)
- DermNet: LV often becomes more frequent during pregnancy — [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/livedoid-vasculopathy)
- German S1 guideline: no statement on infections, vaccination, stress or sleep as triggers; it notes that impaired perfusion favours wound infection — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)

### Inferences
- Systemic infection and inflammation shift haemostasis towards clotting, so infection as a trigger is plausible, and it is a reason to treat foot and leg infections promptly. It is not a reason to avoid vaccination: the vaccine signal is a handful of case reports with no denominator, while infections themselves (including COVID-19) have at least as much case-level evidence as triggers.
- The insect-antigen observation raises the question of whether insect bites on the ankles (more common in summer and in the tropics) act as local triggers. This is speculative.

### Gaps
- No studies on psychological stress, sleep deprivation, shift work, long-haul travel or dehydration as LV triggers.
- No cohort data on vaccination and LV flares (only isolated case reports).
- The Mayo Clinic Connect patient forum contains anecdotes about flares after vaccination and benefits of exercise and elevation; these were seen in search results but are not evidence and were not used.

## 10. Remission on non-drug measures alone, spontaneous remission and "burning out" with age

### Takeaway
Remission without maintenance drug does happen, but in a minority and unpredictably: 5 of 25 followed French patients were in long-term remission off treatment (two after stopping smoking, two after kidney transplantation), about 14% of a large Chinese cohort reached long-term remission, and 10 of 26 patients who stopped rivaroxaban after symptom relief had not relapsed after a median of 17.5 months. No source reports remission attributed to compression, cooling, exercise or other lifestyle measures alone in a non-smoker, and no source shows that LV reliably burns out with age.

### Cited Findings
- French cohort (Gardette et al. 2018; median follow-up 5 years, range 1.2-9.4): long-term remission (no flare for at least 4 years) in 9 patients (36%), median 6 years since last flare. Five of the nine had no maintenance treatment: two remitted after smoking cessation alone, two after renal transplantation for end-stage renal failure, one not described. Lesions disappeared spontaneously in 2 patients. The authors note the benefit of maintenance anticoagulation has not been well evaluated (full text read via summary) — [Gardette et al. 2018](https://www.medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-2965)
- Ban et al. 2026 (236 Chinese patients with follow-up): 34 (14.4%) achieved long-term remission, 55 (23.3%) short-term remission, and 147 (62.3%) had recurrent active ulcers within two years. No significant difference between anticoagulant monotherapy and other regimens. Definitions are inconsistent within the paper: methods define long-term remission as no new ulcer for more than four years; the discussion defines sustained remission as no recurrence four years after treatment discontinuation. Authors conclude anticoagulation alone "may be insufficient ... particularly in preventing recurrence" — [Ban et al. 2026](https://www.dpcj.org/index.php/dpc/article/download/6930/3938/114821)
- Deng et al. 2023 (34 patients on rivaroxaban, mostly 10 mg daily, mean follow-up 32.8 months): 29 stopped the drug (26 after symptom relief, median 9 months on drug). Of those, 16 relapsed, at a median of 7.0 months (IQR 5-12) and mostly mildly (median severity score 2.5); 10 had no recurrence over a median of 17.5 months (IQR 7.5-34.75), two of them relapse-free for over 3 years. 14 resumed rivaroxaban and 12 stopped again after a median second course of 3 months. Five stayed on continuously. "Continuous treatment does not guarantee the absence of relapses." Authors suggest individualised titration "including discontinuation and retreatment". Bleeding: hematochezia in 2 (5.9%), heavy menstrual bleeding in 6 (17.6%) — [Deng et al. 2023, PMC10897505](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10897505/)
- German S1 guideline 2025 treatment algorithm: after healing on low-molecular-weight heparin, reduce dose; if no worsening on reduction, an "Auslassversuch" (trial of stopping); if stable on rivaroxaban, "Rivaroxaban 10 mg/Tag oder Auslassversuch" (10 mg daily or a trial of stopping); on relapse, step back up. Indefinite anticoagulation is therefore not mandated by the guideline for stable patients — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
- Burg et al. 2022: after pain relief, healed ulcers and stable disease, reduce to a maintenance dose "up to an escape attempt"; continue full dose in those at high risk of progression — [Burg et al. 2022, PMC9574051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9574051)
- Seguí and Llamas-Velasco 2022: "Livedoid vasculopathy is a chronic disease with periodic and recurrent exacerbations"; reported remission after rivaroxaban "lasted 4 weeks to 23 months"; ulcers tend to heal slowly over 3-4 months — [Seguí & Llamas-Velasco 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmed.2022.993515)
- DermNet: "a chronic disorder with spontaneous remissions and exacerbations"; reported disease duration has ranged from 2.5 months to 21 years — [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/livedoid-vasculopathy)
- Finnish cohort: 18/24 fully healed, 4 had wounds reopening 2-9 months after healing, 2 never healed — [Köykkä et al. 2026](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/jwm/volume-27-number-2/livedoid-vasculopathy-24-finnish-patients-single-center-institution/10.35279/jowm2026.27.02.14)
- Thai cohort (74 patients, median follow-up 10.5 months): Kaplan-Meier estimates of disease improvement 38.5%, 53.7% and 57.9% at 1, 2 and 3 years (abstract only; "improvement", not remission off treatment) — [Rujitharanawong et al. 2021](https://murex.mahidol.ac.th/en/publications/livedoid-vasculopathy-clinical-course-and-long-term-outcome-in-as/)
- Age: reported median age at diagnosis ranges from 32 to 53 years across series (German guideline); onset peaks before age 20 in the largest Chinese cohort (41.6% under 20; mean disease duration 3.9 years, range 10 days to 30 years), while German and Finnish cohorts have median ages of 53-53.5. Older patients are therefore well represented among active cases — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf); [Ban et al. 2026](https://www.dpcj.org/index.php/dpc/article/download/6930/3938/114821); [Weishaupt et al. 2019](https://read.qxmd.com/read/31009111/characteristics-risk-factors-and-treatment-reality-in-livedoid-vasculopathy-a-multi-centre-analysis)
- Cost of undertreatment: in the French cohort 10 of 20 patients tested had peripheral neuropathy attributed to LV, diagnosed a median 11.4 years after skin onset; the German guideline stresses dysaesthesia as a reason for fast, effective treatment to avert irreversible damage — [Gardette et al. 2018](https://www.medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-2965); [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)

### Inferences
- The realistic alternative to "rivaroxaban indefinitely" that the literature supports is not "non-drug measures instead of the drug" but "supervised stopping trials with fast restart": the German guideline permits an Auslassversuch in stable disease, and Deng's data suggest roughly 4 in 10 who stop stay clear for a year or more, relapses are usually mild, and most respond again on restarting. That is still a drug strategy, with non-drug measures as background.
- The Deng data come from a temperate-to-subtropical region with a cool season; relapses clustered in summer and some settled as it cooled. In a climate without a cool season the off-drug relapse risk may be higher and there is no seasonal window in which stopping is obviously safer. This is an inference.
- A history of more than 10 years of recurrences sits at the long end of reported disease durations (DermNet's 21-year upper figure; Ban's 30-year maximum), which argues against counting on burn-out. Equally, no source shows that long duration predicts permanence; predictors of remission have not been identified in any cohort read.
- The two smoking-cessation remissions are the only reported cases where a lifestyle change alone preceded durable drug-free remission. They are not applicable to a non-smoker, and with two cases and a disease that remits spontaneously, causality is uncertain.
- Neuropathy and scarring are cumulative, so the risk of a failed drug-free trial is not only a new ulcer; this is the main argument for any stopping trial being planned with the dermatologist, with a pain diary and a clear restart threshold.

### Gaps
- No prospective natural-history study of untreated LV, so the true spontaneous remission rate is unknown. Published remission rates are confounded by treatment and by loss to follow-up (34% lost in the largest cohort).
- No study identifies predictors of durable remission (age, sex, duration, thrombophilia status, Lp(a), climate).
- No study compares continuous low-dose anticoagulation with on-demand or seasonal treatment, and none tests whether adding non-drug measures raises the chance of successful withdrawal.
- No data on whether LV activity declines with ageing; the question is not addressed directly in any source found.

## Overall strength-of-evidence summary (for the report writer)

### Takeaway
No non-drug measure has evidence that it can replace antithrombotic treatment in LV. All are adjuncts supported by expert opinion or retrospective association; the strongest signals are for heat as a trigger (consistent observational data, no intervention data) and smoking cessation (two remissions in one cohort).

### Cited Findings
- Evidence grading by measure, compiled from the sources above:
  - Heat avoidance: trigger well documented observationally in several cohorts (China, France, Brazil; absent in Finland); no intervention data — [Ban et al. 2026](https://www.dpcj.org/index.php/dpc/article/download/6930/3938/114821); [Deng et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10897505/); [Köykkä et al. 2026](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/jwm/volume-27-number-2/livedoid-vasculopathy-24-finnish-patients-single-center-institution/10.35279/jowm2026.27.02.14)
  - Light compression (about 20 mmHg, if tolerated, arterial disease excluded): guideline-level expert opinion; no relapse data — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
  - Smoking and nicotine avoidance: expert consensus across reviews plus two off-drug remissions after cessation in one cohort — [Gardette et al. 2018](https://www.medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-2965)
  - Avoiding prolonged standing, leg elevation, regular activity: one small series for standing as aggravator; otherwise extrapolated from venous disease — [Feng et al. 2014](https://medicaljournals.se/acta/content/html/10.2340/00015555-1711); [Phlebolymphology 2025](https://www.phlebolymphology.org/?p=10662)
  - Trauma and footwear protection: expert advice with anecdotal support (2 of 24 patients) — [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/livedoid-vasculopathy)
  - Weight, blood pressure, lipids: described co-factors; no evidence of effect on flares — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
  - Pain diary and early action at the "angina cutis" prodrome: guideline recommendation with strong expert consensus; it guides drug dosing — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
  - Hyperbaric oxygen: small uncontrolled series for healing; relapse common afterwards; not a preventive — [AWMF S1 guideline PDF](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/013-098l_S1_Diagnostik-Therapie-Livedovaskulopathie_2026-05.pdf)
  - Plant-based diet: single case report — [BMJ Case Reports 2021](https://casereports.bmj.com/content/14/2/e237895)
  - Intermittent pneumatic compression, cooling devices, relocation, stress or sleep interventions, alcohol or caffeine restriction: no LV data found.

### Inferences
- Measures with possible harm to flag: compression without arterial assessment or over very painful ulcers; aggressive cooling or ice on poorly perfused skin; contact sport and NSAIDs while anticoagulated; sharp debridement on an anticoagulant; relying on HBO or diet in place of antithrombotic treatment during active disease, given the risk of scarring and neuropathy.
- The patient's existing routine (avoid heat, move regularly, elevate, protect the feet, no smoking, light compression once healed) already covers every non-drug measure that any guideline or review endorses. The literature adds little beyond it apart from: confirming arterial status before compression, a pain diary with an agreed escalation plan, checking homocysteine and Lp(a) if not yet done, prompt treatment of infections, and discussing a supervised stopping trial as the guideline allows.

### Gaps
- The absence of controlled data applies to every row above; a registry or prospective cohort with trigger diaries would be needed to change this.
