# Diagnosis: LV versus small vessel vasculitis

The dermatologist diagnosed livedoid vasculopathy (LV). The biopsy report leaned toward small vessel vasculitis. The history fits LV better, and the response to rivaroxaban is the clearest test.

## What LV is

- Tiny clots block the small blood vessels in the skin of the lower legs and feet. The skin is starved of blood, which causes very painful ulcers that heal slowly and return.
- Healed spots leave white star shaped scars (atrophie blanche) and brown staining.
- It is rare: about 1 new case per 100,000 people a year. It is not contagious and not cancer.
- It runs for years, with flares and quiet periods.
- No medicine is licensed specifically for LV, and there is no cure. Treatment aims to stop pain, heal ulcers and reduce flares.

## What small vessel vasculitis is

- The immune system inflames the walls of small skin vessels.
- It usually shows as crops of raised purple dots that can be felt, often after an infection or a new medicine.
- Most cases settle in weeks. Only about 1 in 10 keeps coming back.
- It can affect the kidneys, joints and gut, so a urine test matters.
- It is treated with anti inflammatory medicines such as colchicine, dapsone or a short course of prednisolone, not blood thinners.

## Informal estimate

| Possibility | Rough chance |
| --- | --- |
| LV, including LV with secondary inflammation | 55 to 70% |
| Primary small vessel vasculitis | 15 to 30% |
| Cutaneous polyarteritis nodosa or another cause | 5 to 15% |

These ranges are reasoning from small case series. No validated score exists. They are not a diagnosis.

### Why LV leads

- In LV biopsy series, neutrophils were seen in about 53% and leaked red cells in about 65%, so inflammation is common in LV.
- In one cohort, about 93% of LV patients first received another diagnosis, most often vasculitis.
- Any ulcer bed shows some nonspecific vasculitis.
- The immunofluorescence pattern (IgM, C3, fibrinogen) is the usual one in LV, though it is also seen in vasculitis.
- Small vessel vasculitis that returns to the same ankle spots for more than a decade is uncommon.

### What argues against LV

- Nuclear dust is the feature most specific to true vasculitis.
- No thickened basement membrane was seen on the special stain. That thickening is often present in LV.
- Eosinophils were present. Arcoxia was started about 8 days before the biopsy, so a drug reaction on top is possible, although the disease is years older than the drug.

### One more possibility

Cutaneous polyarteritis nodosa is inflammation of slightly larger, deeper vessels. It can mimic LV. In one study of 29 patients with white scars and no vein disease, 6 had it, and a small punch biopsy can miss it. Watch for tender lumps under the skin, a net pattern on the skin, or numbness in the feet.

## What would change the estimate

| Finding | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Pain and new ulcers clearly better within 2 to 4 weeks on rivaroxaban | LV rises to about 80 to 85% |
| No meaningful change by about 8 weeks | LV falls to about 35 to 45%. Ask for a deep repeat biopsy and vasculitis tests |
| Repeat biopsy of a fresh spot shows clots without nuclear dust | LV about 85 to 90% |
| Repeat biopsy shows vasculitis in skin that is not ulcerated | Vasculitis rises to about 50 to 60% |
| Abnormal urine, low complement, cryoglobulins or positive ANCA | Vasculitis rises sharply |
| New crops of raised purple dots on both lower legs | Shifts about 10 to 15 percentage points toward vasculitis |

As of 7 Nov, about three and a half weeks into treatment, there were no new spots. That fits LV, but pain scores were not recorded and old wounds were slow to heal.

## What to track

1. Photograph any new spot in its first 1 to 2 days and feel it. Flat and very painful leans LV. Raised dots lean vasculitis.
2. Note whether a flare follows an infection or a new medicine.
3. Score pain from 0 to 10 every week.
4. Watch for joint pain, stomach pain or changes in urine.

## Ways to settle it

- A second biopsy from a fresh spot, not an ulcer, deep enough to include fat.
- Blood and urine tests for vasculitis. See 03_Test_Results.md.
- A second opinion at the National Skin Centre. It is a government specialist centre. AIA's email says the outpatient specialist benefit covers government specialist clinics, subject to limits, so check the remaining balance first.

## Other notes about LV

- **Genes.** LV is not inherited in a simple way. Many patients carry common gene variants that make blood clot more easily (PAI-1 in about 85%, MTHFR in about 55%), but these are also common in healthy people. Gene tests rarely change treatment. The risk to family members is very low.
- **Triggers.** Heat, long standing, knocks to the leg, infections and smoking.
- **Research.** LV is rare, so trials are small. Newer options for stubborn cases include JAK inhibitors (baricitinib, tofacitinib, upadacitinib) and IVIG. Evidence for these is case reports and small series. Trials can be searched at clinicaltrials.gov under "livedoid vasculopathy".
